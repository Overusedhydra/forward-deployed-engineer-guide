Preface

Why This Book Exists

Last summer, my social media feed was flooded with the same number: 95%.

MIT NANDA Lab's 2025 report The GenAI Divide revealed that over the past three years, enterprises worldwide spent $30-40 billion on generative AI, yet 95% of projects failed to produce any value that could appear on a financial statement. Around the same time, another story was racing in the opposite direction: on Silicon Valley job boards, a position called "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) saw postings increase more than sevenfold in a single year.

Core Thesis

Models are no longer scarce — the people who can embed models into real customer businesses are.

I first seriously considered this role while reading Palantir's origin story. Founded in 2003, the company spent twenty years doing something the software industry considers stubborn: sending their best engineers to customer sites — intelligence agencies, battlefields, oil fields, factories — to write code knee-deep in customer data.

Wall Street didn't understand Palantir for years, dismissing it as "more like a consulting company." Then what happened? In 2025, its market cap surpassed $400 billion, and its Rule of 40 metric was so healthy that analysts were rewriting their research reports overnight.

Digging deeper, more clues emerged: OpenAI quietly built an FDE team in 2024; in May 2026, it partnered with 19 top-tier capital firms to launch a "deployment company" valued at $10 billion; Anthropic was reportedly forming a comparable joint venture with Blackstone on nearly the same day.

Key Insight

FDE is not a hiring fad — it is a regime change in how software is delivered.

For the past decade, I've been doing one thing: systematically explaining what's happening in Silicon Valley that doesn't yet have a name in China. FDE sits at a familiar inflection point — Silicon Valley has already written it into org charts, while most Chinese-language discussion still stops at "isn't this just pre-sales with a new name?"

To explain all this, I combed through every primary source I could find: 51-minute podcast retrospectives by early Palantir executives, memoirs by former employees, venture capital industry analyses, the original MIT report, dozens of job postings, salary reports, forum posts by practitioners, and interviews with China's first-wave practitioners.

The Rise of FDE

How a "dumb method" from Palantir's intelligence projects became the hottest role in tech

One challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies.

1.1 First, a Story About a Million Dollars That Died

The beginning of the story is always the same. In a large enterprise's conference room, the vendor's demo has just concluded. The LLM answers fluently, the data dashboard gleams, and even the most demanding executives can't find fault. The CEO makes the decision on the spot: sign. Contract worth millions, handshakes, photos, press release.

Nine months later, the project is dead.

Not dramatically — silently. The system is still running, servers still on, but no business unit is actually using it. The vendor delivered every feature in the contract; the enterprise paid every dollar. The only thing that didn't arrive was "value."

The 95% Problem

MIT NANDA Lab's 2025 report: of $30-40 billion poured into enterprise AI, 95% produced no measurable financial return. This is the norm, not bad luck.

More interesting is the manner of failure. The report specifically notes: the problem isn't the models. Those dazzling models remain smart in production — they just "don't remember feedback, don't retain context, don't enter workflows" — they look like products but feel like exhibits.

It comes down to the same wall: where software is built and where value is created are not in the same place. On this side of the wall, requirements are relayed through tickets, meeting minutes, and weekly reports — each relay distorts a layer; on the other side, the customer's real workflows hide in spreadsheets no one wrote down, in word-of-mouth conventions, in the tacit knowledge of "you need to ask Lao Wang for this."

The Wall

The software industry has invented countless ladders to climb this wall — requirements documents, user research, implementation methodologies, customer success systems — but the wall remains. Until one company decided: we're not climbing the wall anymore; we're sending people over.

1.2 Palantir's Victory

In 2003, Silicon Valley was still climbing out of the internet bubble's ruins. Peter Thiel and a group of Stanford-educated young people founded a company named after a Lord of the Rings artifact — Palantir, the seeing stone that can glimpse distant places.

Our founding goal was to build software for the intelligence community — basically, to build software for spies. And one challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either.

— Bob McGrew on YC's Lightcone podcast

Co-founder Stephen Cohen's solution was adorably crude: build a demo, show it to intelligence people, ask what they think. They were merciless: "This is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat — he asked one more question: "So what would you want it to be different?" Then he took out a notebook, wrote down every item, went back, revised, and brought it back.

The Embryo

This clumsy cycle is the embryo of FDE. Two intuitions: 1) customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something usable; 2) the fastest path to understanding what customers want is to put the people building things next to the people using them.

The one who elevated this intuition into company strategy was the 13th employee, Shyam Sankar. His solution was considered heresy: build a highly customizable platform, then station engineers at customer sites to complete the last mile.

His most critical move was changing the accounting. In the software industry's ledger, "customizing for a single customer" is called services — the enemy of profit margins. Sankar flipped it: on-site customization should be booked as product discovery.

Sankar's Field Experience

Around 2007, Sankar strapped a phone to his head with a rubber band in a classified information room — one ear listening to analysts, the other to Silicon Valley. Nineteen-hour days for two weeks. When it ended, analysts said "this is useful." Sankar called CEO Karp: "This is not sustainable, we're done." Karp's answer: make this "unsustainable" thing into a system.

By 2016, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers once outnumbered platform engineers. Wall Street didn't understand for years, dismissing it as "people sea tactics."

1.5 Everything Speaks Through Results

Palantir executive Mabrey broke it down into two plain questions: "Does it actually work? Does it actually matter?"

The FDE model institutionally guarantees that "results" aren't diluted, through three things:

  • Pricing aligned with results: Sierra charges per "resolved conversation" — no resolution, no charge. Once pricing is bound to results, the delivery team's entire behavior gets re-sorted.
  • Success metrics front-loaded: FDE project's first step isn't writing code — it's defining "what does success look like" with the customer.
  • Final judge is behavioral change: The milestone isn't the day the system goes live — it's the day the customer's team changes how they work.

1.7 How to Hire FDE

Hiring

Hire "curious bulldozers," not "exquisite craftsmen." Danger signals: craftsmen who put code elegance above results, and loyal executors who treat every customer word as scripture.

Interview: "Problem decomposition" — throw a vague, huge real enterprise problem, 60 minutes, no code. Watch the process, not the answer.

Compensation: Engineer salary + floating bonus tied to operating metrics. Don't put hard sales metrics on FDE — that steers behavior toward signing deals, not results.

1.8 How to Become an FDE

Self-test: 25-50% travel is normal. Work rhythm defined by customer urgency. Burnout risk is real.

Chapter 2

Solving the Right Problem

Most enterprise AI failures happen here — not in technology

On the wrong problem, all execution is waste.

2.1 An Autopsy Report on the PoC Graveyard

Silicon Valley has a term: "PoC purgatory" — projects that enter never come out. Not dead enough to declare failure; not alive enough to increase investment.

Five Roadblocks (by frequency)

1. Employees unwilling to use new tools
2. Concerns about model output quality
3. Poor user experience
4. Lack of executive support
5. Change management difficulties

What's absent is more worth noting: models not smart enough, compute not cheap enough, technology not advanced enough — none are listed. What kills these projects almost entirely happens at the "problem definition" and "organizational reality" level.

Key Finding

Projects done with external professional suppliers have about 3x the success rate of internal self-built ones. Why? They can't afford to lose — people paid by results bear the cost of defining the wrong problem themselves.

2.2 PSF: Finding Problem-Solution Fit

PMF asks "does my product have market demand" — supply side. PSF asks "is this specific customer's specific problem worth solving and can it be solved by our capabilities" — demand side.

Three Gates

GateQuestionExample
Gate 1Pain Verification"Improve customer service efficiency" = direction. "Customer service supervisor spends 3 hours every Monday aggregating tickets from 4 systems" = pain point.
Gate 2Economic VerificationHow many person-hours per week? What's the labor cost? What does one error cost?
Gate 3Feasibility VerificationWhere is the data? What condition? What's the accuracy threshold?
Key Insight

Over half of enterprise AI budgets go to front-office sales and marketing, yet returns concentrate in back-office contract review, procurement, and risk control. Everyone is solving "problems that demo well" rather than "problems that are worth money."

2.3 Refusing the Expensive PoC Graveyard

Key Insight

Refusing wrong projects is itself the FDE model's profitability. FDE's cost structure is front-loaded — best engineers, most expensive travel, longest on-site investment, all before payment.

Three Defense Lines

  • PoC Graduation Criteria: Every validation project must specify: after how many weeks, using what metrics, reaching what values → graduate to paid deployment. If not met, part ways cleanly.
  • 3 High-Risk Signals: No-man's land (no business owner), Look-but-don't-touch (no real data), Universal requirements (wants everything in meeting 1). If 2+ appear, be highly alert.
  • Graceful Exit: Translate "not doing it now" into "when to do it." Package rejection as a roadmap.

2.4 Pain Points: The Prime Mover of Deployment

FDE field experience: pain doesn't appear in conference rooms — it only appears at the work site. Palantir wrote this methodology in blood twenty years ago.

Shadow Work

Follow a real user through their real day. Not interviewing them — sitting beside them watching them work. Watch which systems they open, which spreadsheets they copy-paste between, which steps make them frown, which "official processes" they route around.

Focus on observing "workarounds," not "processes." Official flowcharts tell you how the organization "should operate"; workarounds tell you how it "actually operates." Behind every workaround is an unmet pain point, a system failure — and an FDE opportunity.

2.5 Validating Value with MVD

MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment): Using the smallest engineering investment, in the customer's real environment, against the real pain point, to verify that value actually occurs once.

Three Iron Rules

RuleWhat It Means
Rule 1Real data, no exceptions. Validating with sample data is the first brick in the PoC graveyard. Customers must bring their own real business data.
Rule 2Shrink scope, not value. Don't cut features — cut coverage. "Only cover returns and exchanges, but do it end-to-end with no human intervention."
Rule 3Set a hard deadline. MVD's verification cycle should be measured in weeks, not months. Palantir Bootcamp: 1-5 days. Sierra: 4 weeks. Decagon: 4-8 weeks.

2.6 Accommodating the Customer's Environment

FDE practice offers a middle path: "read old, write new."

  • Data level: Deeply accommodate. Read customer data wherever it is — mainframes, spreadsheets, ancient systems.
  • Architecture level: Resolutely don't become a parasite. Validation systems run within self-controlled boundaries, interacting through interfaces.
  • Process level: Follow human habits, not system habits. If users work in spreadsheets and email, MVD's interface should appear there.

2.7 "Actions Speak Louder Than Words" User Research

Traditional research's creed is "ask": surveys, interviews, focus groups. FDE's creed is "watch" and "do."

  • Layer 1: Customers don't know what they want. People's ability to judge "what's wrong" is far stronger than their ability to imagine "what they want."
  • Layer 2: What customers say and what they do are two different things. Only behavior doesn't lie.
  • Layer 3: The highest-quality research happens in joint labor. Those 2-3 days in Bootcamp when FDE and customer technicians write code back-to-back — the information density exceeds any formal research.

3.1 Screening Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson of internet product customer acquisition is screening seed users: 100 users who love you beat 10,000 who think you're okay. The FDE equivalent is lighthouse customers.

Lighthouse Value

1) Strongest sales asset — peer endorsement is the shortest persuasion path. 2) Determines product DNA — first 10 customers participate in shaping your product. 3) Quality > quantity — one elite team can deeply serve single-digit customers simultaneously.

Harvey x Linklaters: First major customer was Linklaters (3,500 lawyers, 43 offices). Once this lighthouse lit up, PwC, Clyde & Co followed. Winning Linklaters was equivalent to getting a pass to the entire elite law firm market.

3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

YC has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." McGrew's precise formulation: the FDE model is doing things that don't scale, at scale.

LayerWhatWhy
Layer 1People must show upTrust is priced by "number of meetings" and "things experienced together"
Layer 2Hands must get dirtyFeel directly translates into solution quality — only by stepping in farm mud do you understand why that perfect mobile interface is unusable in outdoor bright light
Layer 3First be a servant, then a mentorDo the most inconspicuous services first. These "dumb things" buy three strategic assets: organizational map, data environment truth, and "this outsider is one of us" identity certification

3.3 Trust Dividend: The Endorsement Mine

  • Layer 1: Make delivery into "a story worth telling" — specific number, specific person, specific contrast.
  • Layer 2: Turn customer success into the customer's social currency. J.D. Power started running Bootcamps for its own customers after learning from Palantir.
  • Layer 3: Beware the "backfire mechanism" — one high-profile failed delivery spreads far faster than ten successes.

3.5 Technical Content Marketing

FDE company content marketing has three iron rules:

  • Iron rule 1: Write "trench perspective," not "booth perspective." Barry's "self-exposing" article became Palantir model's best evangelism.
  • Iron rule 2: Open-source methodology, create "qualification to be cited." When the industry's customers start using your framework to ask questions, you've gone from supplier to question-setter.
  • Iron rule 3: Make the customer's internal supporter the hero in content.

3.7 Ecosystem Bundling

FDE era's ecosystem strategy borrows three types of partners' shoulders:

  • Cloud vendors and large platforms: AWS, Azure, GCP are themselves one of the total entrances for enterprise AI procurement.
  • Consulting firms and system integrators: OpenAI's Deployment Company's founding partners include Bain & Company, Capgemini, and McKinsey.
  • Customers' customers: J.D. Power started running Bootcamps for its own customers after learning from Palantir — Palantir's capability spilled outward through J.D. Power's customer relationships.

3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Itself Strategy

A qualified Forward Deployed squad can typically deliver no more than two projects simultaneously at high quality. Capacity scarcity can't be avoided; what can be managed is what price you set for this scarcity.

Three Principles

1) Rank by strategic value, not contract amount. 2) Productize "waiting" — give data prep checklists, organizational warming suggestions, lightweight remote diagnostics. 3) Never promise parallelism beyond capacity.

3.9 Writing Proposals and Validation Plans

A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted pyramid structure:

Layer 2Value validation path — acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, exit mechanisms.
Layer 3Delivery method — architecture diagrams with business annotations, milestones with decision points.
Layer 4Risks and countermeasures — "we've thought about how we might die."

3.10 From On-Site to Remote: Hybrid Delivery

FDE delivery has evolved from pure on-site to "remote-first + on-site at key milestones."

4.1 Go-Live Doesn't Equal Activation

In enterprise deployment, activation means the target user group forms stable usage habits in daily work — not applauding at the demo, but the system still being used at high frequency three months later.

The Dual-Track Problem

Only about 40% of enterprises provide official AI tool subscriptions for employees, while up to 90% of employees use personal consumer-grade AI tools for work daily. System went live, but activation never happened.

Enterprise deployments die at the activation stage because of three gates:

  • Not smooth: One extra step beyond old habits and nobody uses it.
  • Not trustworthy: One mistake and trust goes to zero.
  • Irrelevant: Users feel it has nothing to do with them.

4.2 Hotfix Culture

FDE deployment-period iteration is measured in "days" or even "hours": morning, business user says "this output is missing the vendor code field," afternoon the field is added.

Three Execution Essentials

1) Feedback must reach the person writing code directly. 2) Iteration priority ranked by "usage blockage," not "feature importance." 3) At each day's end ask: which moment of the user's tomorrow did today's change make smoother?

4.3 Evaluation Systems

AI system quality is continuous, probabilistic, scenario-dependent. Without evaluation rulers, AI deployment iteration is running blind.

Three Steps

  • Step 1: Grow from real cases — evaluation sets must come from customer's real business material.
  • Step 2: Make the business side the judge — reviewers must include business experts.
  • Step 3: Connect to production loop — continuously collect real input/output, sample-evaluate regularly, alert when scores drop.

4.5 The Long War of Integration

Every FDE veteran has scars from the same war: integration with customer "legacy systems."

US Navy ShipOS

Submarine scheduling: 160 manual hours → under 10 minutes. Material review: weeks → under 1 hour. "This is not a concept, not a pilot, not research — this is already happening." — Secretary of the Navy

Four Strategic Points

  • Treat integration as a campaign, not chores. Draw the system map during entry due diligence, grade and schedule integration risks.
  • Data problems before model problems. "Garbage in, garbage out" — AI connected to ungoverned data sources.
  • Use AI to fight AI's integration war. Browser agents simulating humans to extract data from old systems without interfaces.
  • Know when to route around, not conquer. Some systems' correct solution is "shadow reading" or "declare isolation."

4.6 Change Management

Activation's biggest soft resistance isn't in technology, it's in the organization. Three groups to manage:

GroupRoleManagement
SupportersInternal alliesGive them presentable material, battle achievements, security.
InfluencersUncrowned kingsInvite them to be first testers, take complaints seriously, make their suggestions visible.
Harmed PartiesInterest groups the solution touchesDesign "paths for the harmed" — transform gatekeepers into coaches, let them see their place in the future.
Ultimate Test

After your team withdraws, is the system still being used? If it's bustling when you're present and rapidly cools after you withdraw, that's not activation — that's dancing accompaniment.

4.7 Automating the Delivery Work Itself

FDE's daily work has a startling proportion of templatable repetitive labor. Four types of assets:

  • Deployment templates: Environment setup, permission configuration, monitoring integration packaged into one-click IaC.
  • Integration component library: Connectors for mainstream enterprise systems, written once, reused everywhere.
  • Checklist culture: Security review checklists, launch admission checklists, withdrawal handover checklists.
  • Automated reporting: Weekly reports to customers, field intelligence to the company, semi-automatically generated.

5.2 Performance and Stability

Enterprise users' tolerance for "slow" is higher than consumers, but tolerance for "unreliable" approaches zero. One wrong inventory recommendation's loss may erase a year of system value.

Four Fortifications

  • Clear SLAs, made visible — not just in contracts, but in customer-visible monitoring dashboards.
  • Guardrails for AI's probabilistic nature — uncertain outputs flagged or routed to humans; high-risk actions have human oversight.
  • On-call and response — system down at 2 AM, FDE's reaction speed is the customer's felt temperature.
  • Capacity and cost planning in sync — always run half a step ahead of the usage curve.

5.3 Lossy Service

The wisdom of "lossy" is actively subtracting on three dimensions: features (dare to say no to long-tail requirements), commitments (tiered, not uniformly highest), and costs (proactive usage bill management).

5.4 Guiding New Users

Enterprise system onboarding is "person-to-person transmission" organizational engineering. Three structures:

  • Tiered training: Deep for admins, scenario-based for regular users (30 min max), one-sentence for executives.
  • "Train the trainer" leverage: Identify enthusiastic molecules, cultivate them into internal trainers.
  • Documentation and self-service: Organized by task not feature, embedded in product not standalone.

5.5 Organizational Maintenance

"Supporter departure" is the second biggest killer of enterprise renewals. Defense in three places:

  • Relationship gridding: Beyond one supporter, develop at least two independent relationship lines.
  • Value organizationalization: Rewrite system's value from "supporter's achievement" into "organizational asset."
  • Departure as ritual: Hold a dignified ceremony, immediately start handover with successor.

Epilogue

The Professional Ethics of FDE

FDE's work nature determines what it will see. To do delivery well, you must look at the customer's most real operating data — including the ugly parts; you must map the organization's power structure; you must touch core business processes — including those workarounds wandering in gray zones.

Six Bottom Lines

1. Data sovereignty belongs to the customer.
2. Honestly report results, including bad news.
3. Don't manufacture dependence, don't sell fear.
4. Take the replaced people seriously.
5. Say no to things customers ask for but shouldn't be done.
6. Remember you represent technology itself.

FDE is a young profession, its professional code hasn't been written by anyone. Hope this book is its first boundary marker.
